How to write a contractor bid without underpricing the job

Dec 4, 2026 · 7 minute read · Filed under Trades

I help run a family framing and contracting business, and the jobs that hurt us most were never the hard ones. They were the ones we bid too low because we priced it in our heads, standing in someone's driveway.

A good bid isn't complicated. It just has to include everything the job actually costs. These are the five lines that get missed most.

1. Waste and overage

You never use exactly what's on the plan. Boards split, cuts go wrong, sheets get damaged. Add a waste percentage to your materials, usually somewhere around 10%, more on jobs with lots of angles or cuts.

2. Delivery, dump and disposal fees

Lumber delivery, a dumpster, a dump run. These are real costs and they belong on the bid, not in your pocket.

3. Drive time and setup

If the job is 45 minutes away, that's an hour and a half a day you're paying for. Same with setup and cleanup at the start and end of every day.

4. Permits and inspections

Permit fees, plan review and the time you spend waiting on an inspector all cost money. Find the fee before you bid, not after you win the job.

5. Overhead and profit

Insurance, your truck, your phone, your tools, your license. A job has to pay a share of those, plus an actual profit on top. If your bid only covers labor and materials, you're working for free.

Simple example

Materials $4,000 plus 10% waste = $4,400. Labor $3,200. Delivery and dumpster $350. Permit $180. Subtotal $8,130. Then overhead and profit go on top of that, not inside it.

Put change orders in writing

When the homeowner asks for "one more little thing," write it down with a price and get a signature before you do it. Small free extras are how a profitable job turns into a break-even one.
