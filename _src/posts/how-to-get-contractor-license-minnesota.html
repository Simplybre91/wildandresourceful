You're good at the work. Homeowners keep asking if you can take the whole job. But the second you look up how to get licensed in Minnesota, you land on a state website full of forms, codes and statute numbers.

I help run a family framing and contracting business here in Minnesota, so I've been through this maze. Here's the plain-words version.

First: Minnesota doesn't have a "general contractor license"

This trips up almost everyone. What Minnesota has is a residential building contractor license, issued by the Department of Labor and Industry (DLI). There are also two smaller versions:

LicenseWhat it lets you do
Residential building contractor (BC)Everything, including new homes and detached garages
Residential remodeler (CR)Work on existing homes only
Residential roofer (RR)Roofing only (not gutters, soffit or fascia)

Commercial work is different. It's handled by local cities, not the state.

Do you even need a license?

Generally, yes, if you contract directly with a homeowner on a home with one to four units, and the job covers more than one skill area (say, framing plus drywall plus trim).

If you only work as a sub for a licensed contractor, you usually don't need your own license. There's also an exemption for very small operations. Check DLI's certificate of exemption to see if you qualify.

Why it matters: doing unlicensed work when you need a license is a misdemeanor in Minnesota, and cities can't issue you a building permit without one.

The 10 steps to getting licensed

  1. Pick your business structure and name. In Minnesota, the business gets licensed, not you personally.
  2. File with the state and get your tax numbers.
  3. Choose your qualifying person, the owner or manager who takes the exam for the business.
  4. Pass the exam. More on this below.
  5. Get insured. General liability of at least $100,000 per occurrence and $300,000 total for bodily injury, plus $25,000 for property damage (or a $300,000 single-limit policy).
  6. Apply for the license through DLI.
  7. Write bids and contracts that follow Minnesota law. Everything in writing, including change orders.
  8. Protect your pay with lien notices.
  9. Hire subs and employees the right way, including workers' comp.
  10. Renew and keep up your continuing education: 14 hours every two-year license period.

What's on the Minnesota contractor exam?

  • 110 multiple-choice questions
  • 5.5 hours to finish, and 70% to pass
  • About 60% building code, and the rest on Minnesota laws and rules
  • No experience or school required to sit for it

They hand you the code book and a reference manual during the test. But 110 questions in 5.5 hours is about 3 minutes each. If you have to hunt for every answer, you'll run out of time. The people who pass know the most-tested numbers cold and know which chapter holds everything else.

If you fail, you wait 30 days and submit a new application and fee.

The deadline that costs contractors the most money

It's the pre-lien notice. If you contract directly with a homeowner, the notice has to be in your written contract. With no written contract, you deliver it separately within 10 days after the work is agreed on. Miss it, and you can lose your lien rights.

If a client won't pay, you generally have 120 days after your last day of work to record a lien.
